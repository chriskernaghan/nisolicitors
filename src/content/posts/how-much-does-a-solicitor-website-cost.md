If you ask three web designers what a website costs, you'll get three wildly different numbers, and none of them will mean much without context. So rather than give you a single figure that's useless the moment your needs differ from the next firm's, here's an honest look at what law firms in Northern Ireland actually pay, and what moves the price one way or the other.

## The rough brackets

For a small to mid-sized firm in NI, most website projects fall into one of three brackets.

At the lower end, somewhere around the low thousands, you're looking at a clean, well-built site of five to ten pages. Home, about, your areas of practice, the team, and a contact page. This is right for a lot of firms, and there's no shame in it. A focused site that loads fast and makes it easy to get in touch will do more for you than a sprawling one that nobody finishes building.

In the middle, you're paying for more pages, more bespoke design work, and usually some content help. This suits firms with several distinct practice areas that each deserve their own proper page, or firms that want to publish articles and updates regularly.

At the higher end, you're into custom functionality. Case enquiry forms that route to the right department, client portals, integrations with your practice management software, that sort of thing. Most high-street firms don't need this, and you shouldn't pay for it unless there's a clear reason.

> The most expensive website is the one you have to replace in two years because it was built badly the first time.

## What pushes the price up

A few things reliably move the number upward, and it's worth knowing which of them apply to you so you can decide what's actually worth paying for.

The big one is content. If you can write your own practice-area pages, or already have them, you save a fair bit. If you need someone to research and write them in clear, client-friendly language that also reads well to Google, that's real work and it costs accordingly. For a law firm this is often money well spent, because the writing on a legal site has to be both accurate and approachable, which is harder than it sounds.

Design complexity matters too. A site built on a sensible template, tailored to your firm, costs less than one designed from a blank page. For most firms the tailored-template route gives you ninety per cent of the distinctiveness for a fraction of the cost.

The third factor is everything that happens after launch. A site is not a one-off purchase like a filing cabinet. It needs hosting, updates, security patching and the occasional change. Some designers fold this into an ongoing care plan, others leave you to sort it yourself. Neither is wrong, but you want to know which you're getting before you sign anything.

## What pulls the price down

On the other side, a clear brief is the single biggest saving. If you know what you want, can provide your content, and don't need the moon, a good designer can move quickly and that shows up in the cost.

Working with an independent rather than an agency usually helps as well. With a one-person operation you're not paying for account managers, sales teams and office overheads. You're paying for the actual work. The trade-off is that an independent has finite capacity, so the good ones get booked up, but for a high-street firm that's rarely a problem.

## What a typical NI firm should budget

If you're a small firm wanting a credible, professional site that brings in enquiries and is easy to keep current, budget realistically for the lower-to-middle bracket and treat anything fancier as optional until you've a reason for it. Spend the money on three things in this order: a site that loads fast and works on a phone, writing that speaks plainly to the people you want as clients, and a sensible plan for keeping it maintained.

Everything else is a nice-to-have.
